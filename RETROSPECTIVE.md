RETROSPECTIVE REPORT                                       
Role Rotation: Backend Developer (Weeks 1-3), QA and Integration Tester (Weeks 4-7), API Lead (Weeks 8-10), DevOps (Weeks 11-14)
1. The hardest part of the lifecycle
The hardest stages of the lifecycle for our team were Secure and Monitor, which fell right across my rotations as API Lead in Weeks 8 to 10 and DevOps in Weeks 11 to 14. When I started as Backend Developer in Weeks 1 to 3, our only goal was getting basic CRUD endpoints up and running for our Casual-Worker-Sourcing-App. At that stage, we did not worry much about authentication headers, rate limits, or structured request logging.
The challenge came later when we had to layer security and monitoring onto an application that was already partially built while simultaneously integrating with our partner team. Because Kwezikulu had prior software engineering experience, we did the vast majority of our coding on his laptop. That worked well for building features fast, but when I rotated into DevOps for the final weeks, we realized that having everything living comfortably on Kwezikulu's local environment hid several deployment traps. We had to make sure our .gitignore properly excluded .env and *.log files, verify that no real API keys were hardcoded anywhere in the repo or README.md, and get our Week 12 request logger printing cleanly in the terminal so the class could see live partner calls during Demo Day. Retrofitting clean environment separation and live monitoring at the end of the project was much harder than building the initial routes.
2. One real cross-team issue
One major cross-team issue occurred in Week 10 when I was finishing my rotation as API Lead and handing over to DevOps. We had just enabled API key authentication and updated our integration to point to our partner's v2 endpoints (PARTNER_VERSION=v2), but during a joint test session, every request from our server to their API failed with a 401 Unauthorized status code, while their calls to us were also failing authentication. Because their call was failing, our frontend immediately dropped into our Week 11 fallback mode and displayed saved data.
At first, both teams thought the other side's server was down, which wasted about thirty minutes of back-and-forth messages. Once we turned on our request logs and checked INTEGRATION_LOG.md on Kwezikulu's laptop, we saw the exact 401 entries and realized two separate mistakes had happened at once: we were passing our partner key under the wrong header name after a middleware update, and the partner team was still using an old v1 key from Week 7 that we had rotated after a practice run. Kwezikulu and I fixed our outbound header in the partner service file on his laptop, I issued them a fresh key for their .env, and we documented the header requirement in VERSIONING_NOTES.md. Within an hour, both directions returned 200 OK with "stale": false.
3. What I would design differently
If we started the project over knowing what we know now, I would design the authentication middleware, .env variable naming, and Week 12 logging format in Week 3 before writing any feature endpoints. Adding auth and rate limiting in the middle of the semester forced us to go back and update almost every route and test file, and it caused avoidable friction with our partner team. I would also populate our database with realistic Kenyan casual worker and job records from the very beginning instead of using throwaway strings like "asdf" and "test", which we had to clean out before our pre-flight check.
What I would keep is our Week 12 terminal logging setup and our CONTRACT_DEVIATIONS.md tracking. Having a visible terminal log where every incoming partner request prints the method, path, and status code in real time was the single best debugging tool we had, and it gave us complete confidence for the live demo.
4. My contribution
I would rate my own contribution an 8 out of 10. In Weeks 1 to 3 as Backend Developer, I helped design our initial data models for workers and job listings. In Weeks 4 to 7 as QA and Integration Tester, I tested our early v1 endpoints and logged discrepancies in CONTRACT_DEVIATIONS.md. In Weeks 8 to 10 as API Lead, I coordinated our v2 contract updates and security requirements with our partner team, and in Weeks 11 to 14 as DevOps, I owned the pre-flight checklist, verified git status to ensure .env and *.log files were never committed, and tested our fresh-clone startup script run.sh.
Being honest about team dynamics, Kwezikulu carried the biggest share of the actual coding because of his prior software engineering experience, and most of the codebase was written on his laptop during our group work sessions. Where I contributed strongly was in structuring the backend data early on, keeping our cross-team communication clear when I was API Lead, and making sure our repository, environment variables, and terminal logs were clean and ready for Demo Day.
